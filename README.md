             CARDIOVASCULAR DRUGS
Dosage • Prescription • Administration • Storage • Expiry
An educational quick-reference for pharmacy and healthcare students

Scope	Common adult cardiovascular medicines used for hypertension, angina, heart failure, thrombosis and dyslipidaemia.
Important	Doses below are typical adult examples, not individual prescriptions. Actual treatment depends on diagnosis, BP/HR, renal/hepatic function, age, interactions and local guidelines.
Safety	Prescription cardiovascular medicines should be supplied and monitored by an authorised healthcare professional.

Clinical foundation
WHO identifies major cardiovascular medicines such as diuretics, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and statins among basic medicines used in cardiovascular care. Treatment selection should follow clinical assessment and applicable national/WHO guidance.
 
1. Key Principles of Cardiovascular Medicines
•	Do not start, stop, double or change a cardiovascular medicine without professional advice. Some medicines can cause dangerous rebound effects or deterioration when stopped suddenly.
•	Prescription status varies by country and product. In Uganda, dispensing should follow applicable national medicines regulation and the prescriber's order.
•	Always verify the generic name, strength, dosage form, route, directions, quantity, prescriber details and patient information before dispensing.
•	Storage instructions on the product label/manufacturer's package take priority over a generic storage recommendation.
•	An expiry date means the manufacturer has established stability only through that date under specified storage conditions. Do not use medicines after expiry.
•	Keep medicines in their original labelled containers, protected from heat, moisture and direct sunlight unless the label specifies otherwise.
2. Common Cardiovascular Drug Classes
Class	Examples	Main uses	Prescription	Key safety point
ACE inhibitors	Enalapril, Lisinopril	Hypertension; heart failure; cardiovascular/renal protection in selected patients	Prescription	Monitor BP, renal function and potassium; cough/angioedema can occur.
ARBs	Losartan, Valsartan	Hypertension; heart failure; alternative when ACE-inhibitor cough occurs	Prescription	Monitor BP, renal function and potassium.
Calcium-channel blockers	Amlodipine, Nifedipine	Hypertension; angina	Prescription	Monitor BP; ankle oedema and flushing may occur.
Thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics	Hydrochlorothiazide, Indapamide	Hypertension; fluid control in selected patients	Prescription	Monitor electrolytes, renal function and BP.
Loop diuretics	Furosemide	Oedema/heart failure; fluid overload	Prescription	Monitor fluid status, BP, electrolytes and renal function.
Beta-blockers	Bisoprolol, Metoprolol	Selected hypertension/angina/arrhythmias/heart failure	Prescription	Check pulse/BP; do not stop abruptly.
Nitrates	Glyceryl trinitrate (nitroglycerin)	Rapid relief/prevention of angina	Prescription/controlled according to product and jurisdiction	Never combine with PDE-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil because of potentially severe hypotension.
Antiplatelets	Aspirin, Clopidogrel	Prevention of arterial thrombosis in selected patients	Prescription for many indications	Bleeding risk; aspirin is not appropriate for everyone.
Anticoagulants	Warfarin, Apixaban, Rivaroxaban	Prevention/treatment of selected venous or cardioembolic thrombosis	Prescription	Bleeding risk; interactions and monitoring requirements vary.
Statins	Atorvastatin, Simvastatin	Reduction of LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk	Prescription	Monitor for muscle symptoms and clinically indicated laboratory tests.
 
3. Typical Adult Dose & Administration Reference
The following are common starting/maintenance examples for educational purposes. They are NOT universal prescriptions. The exact dose must be selected for the individual patient.
Medicine	Typical adult dose*	How to take	Prescription	Storage
Amlodipine	5 mg once daily; may be increased to 10 mg once daily	Swallow with water; with or without food.	Usually prescription	Room temperature; protect from excessive heat/moisture.
Enalapril	Hypertension: commonly 5 mg once daily initially; titrated by clinician	With or without food; take consistently.	Prescription	Store in original container at labelled conditions.
Losartan	Hypertension: commonly 50 mg once daily; may be adjusted	With or without food.	Prescription	Store in original container; protect from moisture.
Hydrochlorothiazide	Commonly 12.5–25 mg once daily for hypertension	Usually in the morning to reduce nocturia.	Prescription	Keep dry; follow package storage conditions.
Furosemide	Dose varies widely; often 20–40 mg initially for oedema, then individualized	Usually morning; second dose, if prescribed, earlier in day.	Prescription	Keep dry; monitor fluid/electrolytes.
Bisoprolol	Heart failure: often started very low and titrated; hypertension/angina doses commonly 5–10 mg once daily	Take at same time daily; do not stop abruptly.	Prescription	Room temperature; follow label.
Glyceryl trinitrate SL	For acute angina: commonly 0.3–0.6 mg under tongue per product instructions; repeat only as directed	Sit down; place under tongue; do not swallow. Seek urgent help if chest pain persists as instructed by emergency guidance.	Prescription/varies by product	Protect from heat/light; keep container tightly closed; follow product-specific instructions.
Aspirin (secondary prevention)	Commonly 75–100 mg once daily when specifically indicated	Take with water; food may reduce stomach irritation.	Prescription/varies by indication	Store dry; do not use after expiry.
Clopidogrel	Commonly 75 mg once daily for selected indications	With or without food; take consistently.	Prescription	Store in original pack.
Warfarin	Highly individualized; dose determined by INR and indication	Same time each day; consistent vitamin-K intake; regular INR monitoring.	Prescription	Store at room temperature in original container.
Apixaban	Commonly 5 mg twice daily for many adult indications; dose may be reduced for specific patients	With or without food; doses should be evenly spaced.	Prescription	Store at labelled room-temperature conditions.
Atorvastatin	Commonly 10–20 mg once daily initially, titrated according to risk/response	With or without food; usually once daily.	Prescription	Store in original container; protect from excessive heat/moisture.
*Dose examples are educational only and do not replace the product label, treatment guideline or patient-specific prescription.
 
4. Prescription and Dispensing Checklist
•	Confirm the patient's identity and clinical indication.
•	Check the medicine name, strength, dosage form, route, dose, frequency, duration and quantity.
•	Check allergies, pregnancy status where relevant, kidney/liver function, blood pressure/pulse and clinically important interactions.
•	Review other medicines, including OTC medicines and herbal products.
•	Explain the purpose of the medicine and exactly how and when to take it.
•	Explain common important adverse effects and which symptoms require urgent medical attention.
•	Confirm the patient understands what to do if a dose is missed; do not routinely double the next dose.
•	Document dispensing and provide appropriate follow-up/monitoring instructions.
5. Storage of Cardiovascular Medicines
Product type	Storage principle	Practical advice
Tablets/capsules	Usually at controlled room temperature as stated on label	Keep dry; avoid bathrooms, direct sunlight and excessive heat.
Nitroglycerin/GTN	Product-specific; many formulations require protection from heat/light and tightly closed packaging	Keep in original container and follow manufacturer's instructions exactly.
Liquid medicines	Product-specific	Check label after opening; some liquids have shorter in-use stability.
Refrigerated products	Only when the label specifically requires it	Do not freeze unless explicitly permitted.
All medicines	Follow labelled storage conditions	Keep out of children's reach; do not transfer to unlabelled containers.
6. Expiry Dates: What Patients and Students Must Know
•	EXP 06/2027 generally means the product should be used through the end of June 2027, unless the manufacturer states a different convention.
•	Do not use an expired medicine. Expiration dating reflects the period for which the product is known to retain required strength, quality and purity under specified storage conditions.
•	Heat, humidity, light and poor storage can reduce medicine stability even before the printed expiry date.
•	Never guess an expiry date for a loose tablet removed from its original labelled package.
•	Return expired or unwanted medicines to a pharmacy or approved medicine-disposal service where available; do not keep them for future use.
7. How Patients Should Take Cardiovascular Medicines
•	Take the medicine exactly as prescribed and at the same time each day when possible.
•	Use water unless the medicine label or pharmacist gives different instructions.
•	Do not share medicines with another person, even if they have similar symptoms.
•	Do not stop antihypertensives, beta-blockers, anticoagulants or other long-term cardiovascular medicines abruptly unless directed by a healthcare professional.
•	If a dose is missed, follow the product-specific leaflet or pharmacist's instructions; avoid automatically taking a double dose.
•	Maintain scheduled blood-pressure, pulse, kidney-function, electrolyte, lipid or INR monitoring when required.
 
8. Important Interactions & Red Flags
Combination	Potential concern	Action
ACE inhibitor/ARB + potassium-raising medicines	May increase potassium	Seek pharmacist/clinician review; monitoring may be required.
Nitrate + sildenafil/tadalafil or similar PDE-5 inhibitor	Can cause severe hypotension	Do NOT combine; seek urgent professional advice.
Anticoagulant + NSAID/other bleeding-risk medicines	May increase bleeding	Check with pharmacist/clinician before use.
Beta-blocker + other medicines lowering heart rate	May cause excessive bradycardia/hypotension	Requires professional review.
Diuretic + dehydration/other electrolyte-altering drugs	May disturb electrolytes and renal function	Maintain monitoring and follow clinician advice.
Statin + certain interacting medicines	Can increase statin toxicity/myopathy risk	Check interactions before adding medicines.
9. When to Seek Urgent Medical Care
•	Severe or new chest pain, especially with sweating, shortness of breath, nausea, fainting or pain spreading to the arm/jaw/back.
•	Sudden weakness/numbness on one side, facial drooping, difficulty speaking or sudden severe confusion—possible stroke.
•	Severe difficulty breathing, collapse or loss of consciousness.
•	Major bleeding, vomiting blood, coughing blood, black/tarry stools or severe unexplained bruising while taking antithrombotic medicines.
•	Severe swelling of the lips, tongue or throat after an ACE inhibitor or another medicine.
10. Patient Counselling Points
•	Know the generic name, strength, purpose and dosing schedule of every cardiovascular medicine you take.
•	Keep an updated medicine list and show it to every healthcare professional.
•	Check the expiry date and storage conditions before use.
•	Keep medicines in original labelled packaging where possible.
•	Lifestyle measures remain important: reduce excess salt, be physically active as medically appropriate, avoid tobacco, maintain a healthy weight and follow a heart-healthy diet.
•	Never use someone else's blood-pressure, heart or blood-thinning medicine.
11. Selected References
•	World Health Organization. Guideline for the pharmacological treatment of hypertension in adults (2021/2022).
•	World Health Organization. Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) fact sheet.
•	U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Expiration Dates – Questions and Answers.
•	U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Think It Through: Managing the Benefits and Risks of Medicines.
•	Always consult the current product Summary of Product Characteristics/package insert and applicable Ugandan/national treatment guidelines before prescribing or dispensing.

COMPREHENSIVE ANTIBIOTICS & ANTIMICROBIAL STEWARDSHIP GUIDE

CLINICAL SAFETY: This guide is not a substitute for diagnosis, a patient-specific prescription, a current Uganda Clinical Guideline, antimicrobial susceptibility results, or the manufacturer's product information.
 
1. Clinical Safety and Scope
Antibiotics are medicines used against susceptible bacterial infections. They do not treat viral illnesses such as uncomplicated common colds or most uncomplicated acute bronchitis. The correct antibiotic depends on the suspected or confirmed organism, site of infection, severity, patient factors, local resistance patterns, previous antibiotic exposure and culture/susceptibility results when indicated.
DO NOT SELF-PRESCRIBE: The doses in this document are representative educational reference doses, not automatic prescriptions. They are deliberately presented with context because the same antibiotic can have very different doses, routes and durations for different infections.
The Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023 emphasize using antimicrobials only when necessary, avoiding self-prescription, avoiding unnecessarily broad-spectrum/last-generation antibiotics as first-line therapy, and prescribing correct doses for the correct duration. citeturn2search1
WHO's AWaRe approach groups antibiotics into Access, Watch and Reserve to support stewardship and reduce antimicrobial resistance. citeturn0search5turn0search13
How to use this document
•	Use the class and mechanism sections for learning pharmacology.
•	Use the dose tables as a starting reference only; always verify the indication-specific regimen.
•	For a real patient, confirm age, weight, allergies, pregnancy/breastfeeding status, renal and hepatic function, interacting medicines, severity and relevant investigations.
•	For injectable antibiotics, verify dilution, compatibility, route, infusion time and monitoring requirements from the product information and institutional protocol.
•	For children, calculate doses from the correct weight-based guideline; do not simply scale adult doses.

3. What Antibiotics Are
Antibiotics are antibacterial medicines that inhibit the growth of bacteria or kill susceptible bacteria. They may be bactericidal (kill bacteria) or bacteriostatic (primarily inhibit bacterial growth, allowing host defenses to contribute). Some drugs have concentration-dependent activity, others time-dependent activity, and dosing strategy should reflect the pharmacodynamic target.
Core concepts
Concept	Meaning	Clinical importance
Bactericidal	Produces bacterial killing	Often important in severe infections, endocarditis and some immunocompromised patients, depending on organism/drug.
Bacteriostatic	Primarily inhibits growth	Clinical effectiveness still depends on drug exposure and host immunity.
Broad spectrum	Active against many bacterial groups	Useful when justified, but unnecessary exposure increases adverse effects and selection pressure.
Narrow spectrum	Targets a smaller range of organisms	Preferred when the pathogen is known and susceptibility allows.
Empiric therapy	Treatment started before the organism is confirmed	Based on syndrome, severity, likely organisms and local resistance.
Definitive therapy	Treatment selected after microbiology results	Allows narrowing/de-escalation where appropriate.

When antibiotics may be inappropriate
•	Most uncomplicated viral upper-respiratory infections.
•	Most uncomplicated acute bronchitis.
•	Symptoms where bacterial infection has not been established and clinical assessment supports non-antibiotic management.
•	Use merely to satisfy a patient's expectation when there is no bacterial indication.
CDC notes that antibiotics are not routinely recommended for uncomplicated acute bronchitis and that most acute rhinosinusitis cases are viral. citeturn0search1
 
4. Major Antibiotic Classes and Mechanisms
Class	Examples	Main target/mechanism	Important notes
Penicillins	Amoxicillin, benzylpenicillin, phenoxymethylpenicillin	β-lactam inhibition of bacterial cell-wall synthesis	Allergy history is important; resistance commonly involves β-lactamases or altered PBPs.
β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations	Amoxicillin/clavulanate, piperacillin/tazobactam	Cell-wall inhibition plus enzyme inhibition	Broader spectrum; use only when indicated.
Cephalosporins	Cefalexin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone	β-lactam cell-wall inhibition	Generation affects spectrum; allergy/cross-reactivity assessment matters.
Macrolides	Azithromycin	50S ribosomal inhibition	QT prolongation and interactions are important for some patients.
Tetracyclines	Doxycycline	30S ribosomal inhibition	Avoid/limit in pregnancy and young children depending on agent and indication.
Fluoroquinolones	Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin	DNA gyrase/topoisomerase inhibition	Reserve for appropriate indications because of resistance and potentially serious adverse effects.
Aminoglycosides	Gentamicin, amikacin	30S ribosomal inhibition	Renal and auditory toxicity; therapeutic monitoring may be required.
Nitroimidazoles	Metronidazole	Damages DNA in susceptible anaerobes/protozoa	Avoid alcohol during treatment and for the product-specific period after completion.
Carbapenems	Meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin	Broad β-lactam cell-wall inhibition	Reserve/specialist agents for serious resistant infections.
Glycopeptides	Vancomycin	Inhibits cell-wall synthesis	Primarily for Gram-positive organisms; IV use requires monitoring in many settings.

Bacteriological targets
•	Cell-wall synthesis: β-lactams and glycopeptides.
•	Protein synthesis: macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides.
•	Nucleic-acid synthesis: fluoroquinolones and rifamycins.
•	Folate pathway: trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole.
•	Membrane/DNA effects: metronidazole and selected other agents.
 
5. WHO AWaRe Stewardship Framework
Group	Purpose	Typical approach
ACCESS	Generally preferred first-line agents for many common infections; usually lower resistance potential than many Watch agents.	Use when clinically appropriate; optimize dose and duration.
WATCH	Higher resistance potential and/or important stewardship considerations.	Use for specific indications with careful justification.
RESERVE	Last-line/critical options for multidrug-resistant infections.	Use when alternatives are inappropriate or ineffective, usually with specialist/institutional oversight.

WHO describes AWaRe as a tool to optimize antibiotic use and curb antimicrobial resistance. citeturn0search13
 
 
6. Common Antibiotics: Reference Dosing and Practical Use
The following are representative adult and/or pediatric reference examples. They are not a universal prescription chart. Doses and durations vary substantially by infection and guideline.
Antibiotic	Common reference adult regimen*	Selected pediatric reference*	Administration / key cautions
Amoxicillin	Commonly 500 mg PO every 8 h; some infections require higher doses	Often 25–50 mg/kg/day divided doses; infection-specific maximums apply	Check penicillin allergy; may be taken with or without food depending on product.
Amoxicillin/clavulanate	Dose depends on formulation and infection; commonly 625 mg (500/125) PO every 8 h in some adult regimens	Weight-based dosing uses the amoxicillin component; formulation matters	Take with food to reduce GI upset; avoid confusing different strengths.
Benzylpenicillin	Serious infections require IV/IM dosing determined by indication and guideline	Weight-based, indication-specific	Parenteral; severe allergy risk.
Phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V)	Commonly 500 mg PO 2–4 times daily for selected infections	Weight/age-based	Best taken as directed in relation to food; indication-specific duration.
Cefalexin	Often 500 mg PO every 6–12 h depending on infection	Typically 25–50 mg/kg/day divided; severe infections may require higher guideline doses	Cephalosporin allergy considerations; renal adjustment may be needed.
Ceftriaxone	Commonly 1–2 g IV/IM once daily; severe infections may require indication-specific regimens	Often 50–100 mg/kg/day depending on indication	Hospital/clinical setting; check calcium incompatibility warnings and product instructions.
Azithromycin	Common regimens include 500 mg PO once daily for 3 days or 500 mg day 1 then 250 mg daily days 2–5, depending on indication	Weight-based and indication-specific	QT prolongation risk; do not use routinely for infections where resistance makes it inappropriate.
Ciprofloxacin	Often 500 mg PO every 12 h for selected infections	Not routinely used in children except specific indications under specialist guidance	Tendon/neurologic effects, QT issues, interactions with polyvalent cations; renal adjustment.
Metronidazole	Commonly 400–500 mg PO every 8–12 h depending on indication	Weight-based, indication-specific	Avoid alcohol during treatment and for the product-specific post-treatment interval.
Doxycycline	Commonly 100 mg PO every 12 h for selected infections	Generally avoided in young children unless specific specialist indications apply	Photosensitivity; separate from iron/calcium/antacids; pregnancy considerations.
Gentamicin	Dose commonly weight- and renal-function-based; extended-interval regimens are often used	Weight-based and specialist-guided	Nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity; monitoring often required.
Meropenem	Commonly 1 g IV every 8 h for selected severe infections; indication-specific	Weight-based; specialist/hospital use	Reserve-type broad-spectrum agent; renal adjustment.
Vancomycin	Dose based on weight, renal function and monitoring strategy	Weight-based and specialist-guided	Therapeutic drug monitoring often required; infusion reactions possible.

*Educational reference examples only. Exact dose, frequency, route and duration must be selected for the diagnosed infection and patient. The Uganda EMHSLU 2023 lists many of these medicines and their available strengths/forms in Uganda. citeturn2search5
EXPIRY: Never infer an antibiotic's expiry date from the drug name or strength. Expiry is product/lot-specific and depends on validated stability under labeled storage conditions.
 
7. Infection-Based Prescribing Overview
A drug should not be selected simply because a patient has 'fever', 'cough' or 'pain'. The syndrome must be assessed, and serious infection should be recognized early.
Syndrome / condition	Antibiotic approach	Important prescribing point
Acute uncomplicated bronchitis	Usually no antibiotic	Colored sputum alone does not prove bacterial infection. Rule out pneumonia when indicated. citeturn0search1
Suspected bacterial sinusitis	Amoxicillin or amoxicillin/clavulanate are common first-line options in adult guidance	Diagnosis is clinical and should distinguish severe, persistent or worsening disease from viral illness. citeturn0search1
Streptococcal pharyngitis	Penicillin/amoxicillin are standard first-line options when confirmed/appropriately diagnosed	Do not treat viral sore throat with antibiotics.
Uncomplicated cystitis	Use local guideline-selected agents based on patient factors and resistance	Pregnancy changes antibiotic choice and follow-up.
Pneumonia	Choice depends on severity, setting, age, comorbidities and likely organisms	Severe disease may require hospital assessment and parenteral therapy.
Skin/soft tissue infection	Choice depends on organism, purulence, severity and local resistance	Drainage is essential for many abscesses; antibiotics alone may be inadequate.
Meningitis/sepsis	Emergency management; prompt parenteral antibiotics plus supportive care	Do not delay urgent referral/hospital management for self-treatment.
Gonorrhoea/chlamydia and other STIs	Use current national/STI-specific protocols	Resistance is important; partner management and testing are essential.

Uganda-specific principle
Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023 explicitly advises using antimicrobials only when necessary, avoiding self-prescription, avoiding broad/last-generation antibiotics as first-line treatment, and prescribing correct doses for correct duration. citeturn2search1
 
8. Prescription and Dispensing Requirements
A safe antibiotic prescription should allow another health professional to understand exactly what is intended. A complete prescription normally includes:
•	Patient name and identifying details appropriate to the facility.
•	Date of prescription.
•	Diagnosis/clinical indication where appropriate and permitted.
•	Generic name of the antibiotic, formulation and strength.
•	Dose per administration.
•	Route of administration.
•	Frequency and/or interval.
•	Duration or total quantity.
•	Specific instructions such as relation to food, water, separation from minerals/antacids, or completion of the prescribed course.
•	Prescriber identification, signature/authentication and professional details according to applicable local requirements.
•	Relevant allergy or safety information should be documented in the clinical record.
Prescription-writing example — educational only
Field	Example
Medicine	Amoxicillin 500 mg capsules
Sig	Take 1 capsule by mouth every 8 hours
Duration	5 days — only if this duration is appropriate for the diagnosed condition
Quantity	15 capsules
Patient instruction	Take exactly as directed; do not share; return if worsening or serious reaction occurs.
Prescriber	Name / professional registration / signature according to facility policy

EDUCATIONAL EXAMPLE: The example above is a formatting demonstration, not a recommendation to treat an unspecified patient with amoxicillin for five days.
 
9. Guidance for Taking Oral Antibiotics
•	Take the medicine at the prescribed times. Regular intervals help maintain effective drug exposure.
•	Use a proper oral syringe or measuring device for liquid medicines rather than a household spoon.
•	Swallow capsules/tablets with an adequate amount of water unless the product information says otherwise.
•	Follow food instructions on the dispensing label. Some antibiotics can be taken with food; others have important food/mineral interactions.
•	Do not share antibiotics with family members or friends.
•	Do not save leftover antibiotics for a future illness.
•	Do not stop, extend or change the regimen without advice from the prescriber/pharmacist, except where urgent adverse-reaction management requires stopping and seeking care.
•	Keep the medicine in its labeled container with the dispensing label intact.
•	Complete the prescribed course when directed; however, duration should be evidence-based rather than automatically 'as long as possible'.
•	Attend review when instructed, particularly for severe infection, pregnancy, children, recurrent infection or lack of improvement.
Timing examples
Frequency	Approximate spacing	Practical example*
Once daily	24 hours	08:00 each day
Every 12 hours	12 hours	08:00 and 20:00
Every 8 hours	8 hours	06:00, 14:00, 22:00
Every 6 hours	6 hours	06:00, 12:00, 18:00, 00:00

*Actual timing should fit the prescription, patient's routine, drug pharmacology and clinical setting.
 
10. Missed Doses, Vomiting and Treatment Failure
If a dose is missed
•	Take it when remembered unless it is nearly time for the next dose.
•	Do not routinely double the next dose to compensate.
•	For complex regimens, consult the pharmacist/prescriber or product-specific instructions.
If vomiting occurs
•	Do not automatically repeat a dose. Whether to repeat depends on the medicine, time since administration, formulation and whether the dose was visibly lost. Seek professional advice if uncertain.
Possible treatment failure
•	No clinical improvement within the expected timeframe.
•	Symptoms become worse or new severe symptoms appear.
•	Persistent fever, confusion, breathing difficulty, dehydration, severe pain or inability to take oral medication.
•	Culture/susceptibility results show resistance.
•	Poor adherence, incorrect dose, poor absorption, drug interaction or wrong diagnosis.
IMPORTANT: Treatment failure should trigger reassessment rather than simply adding a second antibiotic. Consider diagnosis, source control, adherence, susceptibility and complications.
 
11. Storage, Stability and Expiry Dates
 
Storage principles
Product type	General principle	Critical caution
Tablets/capsules	Store according to label, protected from excessive heat, moisture and light when specified.	Do not transfer into unlabelled containers.
Oral suspensions	Follow the product's reconstitution and storage instructions exactly.	The post-reconstitution shelf-life may differ from the unopened product expiry.
Injectable powders	Store unopened as labeled; after reconstitution/dilution, use the specified stability period.	Compatibility, diluent and temperature matter.
Refrigerated products	Maintain the labeled temperature range and monitor cold chain where required.	Do not freeze unless the product specifically permits it.
All antibiotics	Keep in original packaging and away from children.	Do not use products that are expired, damaged, contaminated or improperly stored.

Understanding expiry dates
An expiration date is the period during which the product is expected to retain its strength, quality and purity when stored under the labeled conditions. FDA explains that expiration dates are supported by stability data and that storage conditions, temperature, humidity and light can affect stability. citeturn0search0turn0search8
•	EXP 2028-06-30 means do not use after the stated date.
•	If only month/year are printed, follow the manufacturer's labeling convention and local regulatory requirements rather than guessing.
•	Do not confuse manufacturing date (MFG) with expiry date (EXP).
•	Lot/batch number is essential for product traceability and recalls.
•	An opened/reconstituted product can have a shorter usable period than the original package expiry.
•	Never extend an expiry date merely because the medicine looks normal.
DO NOT USE EXPIRED ANTIBIOTICS: Expired or degraded antibiotics may be less effective; sub-potent antibiotic exposure can contribute to treatment failure and resistance. citeturn0search9
 
12. Adverse Effects and Serious Reactions
Reaction	Examples	What to do
Common GI effects	Nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea	Hydration and monitoring; seek advice if severe/persistent.
Mild rash	May occur with several antibiotics	Assess carefully; a rash can also signal allergy or a serious reaction.
Immediate hypersensitivity	Hives, facial swelling, wheeze, difficulty breathing, collapse	Emergency treatment/referral; do not re-expose the patient without specialist assessment.
C. difficile-associated diarrhea	Significant or persistent diarrhea after antibiotics	Prompt medical assessment; avoid self-treating severe diarrhea with antidiarrheals without advice.
Tendon injury	Associated especially with fluoroquinolones	Stop and seek medical assessment if severe tendon pain/swelling occurs.
Kidney toxicity	Aminoglycosides and some other agents	Monitor renal function when indicated.
Liver injury	Several antibiotics can cause hepatotoxicity	Assess jaundice, dark urine, severe fatigue or upper abdominal symptoms urgently.
QT prolongation	Macrolides, fluoroquinolones and others	Assess interacting drugs and cardiac risk where relevant.

Emergency warning signs
•	Difficulty breathing or swallowing
•	Swelling of face/lips/tongue
•	Fainting/collapse
•	Severe blistering or peeling skin
•	Severe persistent diarrhea with dehydration or blood
•	Confusion, severe weakness or rapidly worsening infection

Antibiotic	Important interaction/administration issue	Counselling
Doxycycline	Reduced absorption with calcium, iron, magnesium/aluminium antacids	Separate doses according to product-specific instructions; take with adequate water.

14. Special Populations
Patient group	Key considerations
Children	Use current pediatric guidelines and weight-based dosing. Check maximum doses, formulation concentration and measuring device.
Pregnancy	Choose agents with an established safety profile for the specific infection; avoid potentially harmful agents when alternatives exist.
Breastfeeding	Consider infant exposure, age and drug-specific safety.
Renal impairment	Many antibiotics require dose or interval adjustment. Some need therapeutic drug monitoring.
Hepatic impairment	Some antibiotics need caution or adjustment because of hepatic metabolism/toxicity.
Older adults	Higher risk of renal impairment, polypharmacy and adverse drug reactions.
Immunocompromised patients	Threshold for investigation, escalation and specialist care is lower.
Severe infection/sepsis	Urgent assessment, cultures when appropriate, prompt effective therapy and source control are priorities.

IMPORTANT: For a real patient, 'adult dose' is not enough information. Weight, renal function, indication and severity can materially change the regimen.
 
15. Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR)
AMR occurs when microorganisms develop or acquire mechanisms that reduce susceptibility to medicines. Antibiotic exposure creates selection pressure: susceptible bacteria are inhibited while resistant organisms can survive and multiply.
 
Drivers of resistance
•	Unnecessary antibiotic use.
•	Incorrect antibiotic choice.
•	Incorrect dose or interval.
•	Poor-quality/substandard medicines.
•	Poor adherence or inappropriate discontinuation.
•	Routine use of broad-spectrum or Reserve agents when narrower options are effective.
•	Transmission of resistant organisms in healthcare and communities.
•	Poor infection prevention, sanitation and hygiene.
Stewardship actions
•	Confirm that an antibiotic is indicated.
•	Obtain cultures before treatment when clinically appropriate and without unsafe delay.
•	Choose the narrowest effective agent when evidence allows.
•	Use the correct dose, route and duration.
•	Review therapy when microbiology results become available.
•	De-escalate or stop therapy when appropriate.
•	Document indication and duration.
•	Educate patients not to self-medicate or share antibiotics.
WHO and Uganda Ministry of Health both emphasize antimicrobial stewardship and appropriate antibiotic use as strategies to reduce resistance. citeturn0search11turn2search1
 
16. Antibiotic Stewardship in Pharmacy Practice
Pharmacist/dispensing professional checklist
•	Confirm prescription completeness and legality under local policy.
•	Verify patient identity and age/weight where relevant.
•	Ask about allergies and previous serious reactions.
•	Check indication and whether antibiotic therapy is clinically plausible.
•	Check dose, frequency, route, duration and formulation.
•	Check renal/hepatic function where relevant.
•	Screen for interactions and contraindications.
•	Check expiry date, batch number, packaging integrity and storage.
•	Give clear instructions using simple language.
•	Use teach-back: ask the patient to explain how they will take the medicine.
•	Explain common adverse effects and emergency warning signs.
•	Explain storage and what to do with leftover/expired medicine.
•	Document interventions and communicate with the prescriber when clarification is required.
Teach-back example
Instead of asking, 'Do you understand?', ask: 'Just so I know I explained it clearly, can you tell me how you will take these capsules and what you will do if you miss a dose?'
