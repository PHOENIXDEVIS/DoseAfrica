Clinical Safety and Scope Antibiotics are medicines used against susceptible bacterial infections. They do not treat viral illnesses such as uncomplicated common colds or most uncomplicated acute bronchitis. The correct antibiotic depends on the suspected or confirmed organism, site of infection, severity, patient factors, local resistance patterns, previous antibiotic exposure and culture/susceptibility results when indicated. DO NOT SELF-PRESCRIBE: The doses in this document are representative educational reference doses, not automatic prescriptions. They are deliberately presented with context because the same antibiotic can have very different doses, routes and durations for different infections. The Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023 emphasize using antimicrobials only when necessary, avoiding self-prescription, avoiding unnecessarily broad-spectrum/last-generation antibiotics as first-line therapy, and prescribing correct doses for the correct duration. citeturn2search1 WHO's AWaRe approach groups antibiotics into Access, Watch and Reserve to support stewardship and reduce antimicrobial resistance. citeturn0search5turn0search13 How to use this document • Use the class and mechanism sections for learning pharmacology. • Use the dose tables as a starting reference only; always verify the indication-specific regimen. • For a real patient, confirm age, weight, allergies, pregnancy/breastfeeding status, renal and hepatic function, interacting medicines, severity and relevant investigations. • For injectable antibiotics, verify dilution, compatibility, route, infusion time and monitoring requirements from the product information and institutional protocol. • For children, calculate doses from the correct weight-based guideline; do not simply scale adult doses.   2. What Antibiotics Are Antibiotics are antibacterial medicines that inhibit the growth of bacteria or kill susceptible bacteria. They may be bactericidal (kill bacteria) or bacteriostatic (primarily inhibit bacterial growth, allowing host defenses to contribute). Some drugs have concentration-dependent activity, others time-dependent activity, and dosing strategy should reflect the pharmacodynamic target. Core concepts Concept Meaning Clinical importance Bactericidal Produces bacterial killing Often important in severe infections, endocarditis and some immunocompromised patients, depending on organism/drug. Bacteriostatic Primarily inhibits growth Clinical effectiveness still depends on drug exposure and host immunity. Broad spectrum Active against many bacterial groups Useful when justified, but unnecessary exposure increases adverse effects and selection pressure. Narrow spectrum Targets a smaller range of organisms Preferred when the pathogen is known and susceptibility allows. Empiric therapy Treatment started before the organism is confirmed Based on syndrome, severity, likely organisms and local resistance. Definitive therapy Treatment selected after microbiology results Allows narrowing/de-escalation where appropriate. When antibiotics may be inappropriate • Most uncomplicated viral upper-respiratory infections. • Most uncomplicated acute bronchitis. • Symptoms where bacterial infection has not been established and clinical assessment supports non-antibiotic management. • Use merely to satisfy a patient's expectation when there is no bacterial indication. CDC notes that antibiotics are not routinely recommended for uncomplicated acute bronchitis and that most acute rhinosinusitis cases are viral. citeturn0search1   4. Bacteriological targets • Cell-wall synthesis: β-lactams and glycopeptides. • Protein synthesis: macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides. • Nucleic-acid synthesis: fluoroquinolones and rifamycins. • Folate pathway: trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. • Membrane/DNA effects: metronidazole and selected other agents.   5. WHO AWaRe Stewardship Framework Group Purpose Typical approach ACCESS Generally preferred first-line agents for many common infections; usually lower resistance potential than many Watch agents. Use when clinically appropriate; optimize dose and duration. WATCH Higher resistance potential and/or important stewardship considerations. Use for specific indications with careful justification. RESERVE Last-line/critical options for multidrug-resistant infections. Use when alternatives are inappropriate or ineffective, usually with specialist/institutional oversight. WHO describes AWaRe as a tool to optimize antibiotic use and curb antimicrobial resistance. citeturn0search13   6. Infection-Based Prescribing Overview A drug should not be selected simply because a patient has 'fever', 'cough' or 'pain'. The syndrome must be assessed, and serious infection should be recognized early. Syndrome / condition Antibiotic approach Important prescribing point Acute uncomplicated bronchitis Usually no antibiotic Colored sputum alone does not prove bacterial infection. Rule out pneumonia when indicated. citeturn0search1 Suspected bacterial sinusitis Amoxicillin or amoxicillin/clavulanate are common first-line options in adult guidance Diagnosis is clinical and should distinguish severe, persistent or worsening disease from viral illness. citeturn0search1 Streptococcal pharyngitis Penicillin/amoxicillin are standard first-line options when confirmed/appropriately diagnosed Do not treat viral sore throat with antibiotics. Uncomplicated cystitis Use local guideline-selected agents based on patient factors and resistance Pregnancy changes antibiotic choice and follow-up. Pneumonia Choice depends on severity, setting, age, comorbidities and likely organisms Severe disease may require hospital assessment and parenteral therapy. Skin/soft tissue infection Choice depends on organism, purulence, severity and local resistance Drainage is essential for many abscesses; antibiotics alone may be inadequate. Meningitis/sepsis Emergency management; prompt parenteral antibiotics plus supportive care Do not delay urgent referral/hospital management for self-treatment. Gonorrhoea/chlamydia and other STIs Use current national/STI-specific protocols Resistance is important; partner management and testing are essential. Uganda-specific principle Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023 explicitly advises using antimicrobials only when necessary, avoiding self-prescription, avoiding broad/last-generation antibiotics as first-line treatment, and prescribing correct doses for correct duration. citeturn2search1   8. Prescription and Dispensing Requirements A safe antibiotic prescription should allow another health professional to understand exactly what is intended. A complete prescription normally includes: • Patient name and identifying details appropriate to the facility. • Date of prescription. • Diagnosis/clinical indication where appropriate and permitted. • Generic name of the antibiotic, formulation and strength. • Dose per administration. • Route of administration. • Frequency and/or interval. • Duration or total quantity. • Specific instructions such as relation to food, water, separation from minerals/antacids, or completion of the prescribed course. • Prescriber identification, signature/authentication and professional details according to applicable local requirements. • Relevant allergy or safety information should be documented in the clinical record. Prescription-writing example — educational only Field Example Medicine Amoxicillin 500 mg capsules Sig Take 1 capsule by mouth every 8 hours Duration 5 days — only if this duration is appropriate for the diagnosed condition Quantity 15 capsules Patient instruction Take exactly as directed; do not share; return if worsening or serious reaction occurs. Prescriber Name / professional registration / signature according to facility policy EDUCATIONAL EXAMPLE: The example above is a formatting demonstration, not a recommendation to treat an unspecified patient with amoxicillin for five days.   9. Guidance for Taking Oral Antibiotics • Take the medicine at the prescribed times. Regular intervals help maintain effective drug exposure. • Use a proper oral syringe or measuring device for liquid medicines rather than a household spoon. • Swallow capsules/tablets with an adequate amount of water unless the product information says otherwise. • Follow food instructions on the dispensing label. Some antibiotics can be taken with food; others have important food/mineral interactions. • Do not share antibiotics with family members or friends. • Do not save leftover antibiotics for a future illness. • Do not stop, extend or change the regimen without advice from the prescriber/pharmacist, except where urgent adverse-reaction management requires stopping and seeking care. • Keep the medicine in its labeled container with the dispensing label intact. • Complete the prescribed course when directed; however, duration should be evidence-based rather than automatically 'as long as possible'. • Attend review when instructed, particularly for severe infection, pregnancy, children, recurrent infection or lack of improvement. Timing examples Frequency Approximate spacing Practical example* Once daily 24 hours 08:00 each day Every 12 hours 12 hours 08:00 and 20:00 Every 8 hours 8 hours 06:00, 14:00, 22:00 Every 6 hours 6 hours 06:00, 12:00, 18:00, 00:00 *Actual timing should fit the prescription, patient's routine, drug pharmacology and clinical setting.   10. Missed Doses, Vomiting and Treatment Failure If a dose is missed • Take it when remembered unless it is nearly time for the next dose. • Do not routinely double the next dose to compensate. • For complex regimens, consult the pharmacist/prescriber or product-specific instructions. If vomiting occurs • Do not automatically repeat a dose. Whether to repeat depends on the medicine, time since administration, formulation and whether the dose was visibly lost. Seek professional advice if uncertain. Possible treatment failure • No clinical improvement within the expected timeframe. • Symptoms become worse or new severe symptoms appear. • Persistent fever, confusion, breathing difficulty, dehydration, severe pain or inability to take oral medication. • Culture/susceptibility results show resistance. • Poor adherence, incorrect dose, poor absorption, drug interaction or wrong diagnosis. IMPORTANT: Treatment failure should trigger reassessment rather than simply adding a second antibiotic. Consider diagnosis, source control, adherence, susceptibility and complications.   11. Storage, Stability and Expiry Dates Storage principles Product type General principle Critical caution Tablets/capsules Store according to label, protected from excessive heat, moisture and light when specified. Do not transfer into unlabelled containers. Oral suspensions Follow the product's reconstitution and storage instructions exactly. The post-reconstitution shelf-life may differ from the unopened product expiry. Injectable powders Store unopened as labeled; after reconstitution/dilution, use the specified stability period. Compatibility, diluent and temperature matter. Refrigerated products Maintain the labeled temperature range and monitor cold chain where required. Do not freeze unless the product specifically permits it. All antibiotics Keep in original packaging and away from children. Do not use products that are expired, damaged, contaminated or improperly stored. Understanding expiry dates An expiration date is the period during which the product is expected to retain its strength, quality and purity when stored under the labeled conditions. FDA explains that expiration dates are supported by stability data and that storage conditions, temperature, humidity and light can affect stability. citeturn0search0turn0search8 • EXP 2028-06-30 means do not use after the stated date. • If only month/year are printed, follow the manufacturer's labeling convention and local regulatory requirements rather than guessing. • Do not confuse manufacturing date (MFG) with expiry date (EXP). • Lot/batch number is essential for product traceability and recalls. • An opened/reconstituted product can have a shorter usable period than the original package expiry. • Never extend an expiry date merely because the medicine looks normal. DO NOT USE EXPIRED ANTIBIOTICS: Expired or degraded antibiotics may be less effective; sub-potent antibiotic exposure can contribute to treatment failure and resistance. citeturn0search9   12. Adverse Effects and Serious Reactions Reaction Examples What to do Common GI effects Nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea Hydration and monitoring; seek advice if severe/persistent. Mild rash May occur with several antibiotics Assess carefully; a rash can also signal allergy or a serious reaction. Immediate hypersensitivity Hives, facial swelling, wheeze, difficulty breathing, collapse Emergency treatment/referral; do not re-expose the patient without specialist assessment. C. difficile-associated diarrhea Significant or persistent diarrhea after antibiotics Prompt medical assessment; avoid self-treating severe diarrhea with antidiarrheals without advice. Tendon injury Associated especially with fluoroquinolones Stop and seek medical assessment if severe tendon pain/swelling occurs. Kidney toxicity Aminoglycosides and some other agents Monitor renal function when indicated. Liver injury Several antibiotics can cause hepatotoxicity Assess jaundice, dark urine, severe fatigue or upper abdominal symptoms urgently. QT prolongation Macrolides, fluoroquinolones and others Assess interacting drugs and cardiac risk where relevant. Emergency warning signs • Difficulty breathing or swallowing • Swelling of face/lips/tongue • Fainting/collapse • Severe blistering or peeling skin • Severe persistent diarrhea with dehydration or blood • Confusion, severe weakness or rapidly worsening infection   13. Special Populations Patient group Key considerations Children Use current pediatric guidelines and weight-based dosing. Check maximum doses, formulation concentration and measuring device. Pregnancy Choose agents with an established safety profile for the specific infection; avoid potentially harmful agents when alternatives exist. Breastfeeding Consider infant exposure, age and drug-specific safety. Renal impairment Many antibiotics require dose or interval adjustment. Some need therapeutic drug monitoring. Hepatic impairment Some antibiotics need caution or adjustment because of hepatic metabolism/toxicity. Older adults Higher risk of renal impairment, polypharmacy and adverse drug reactions. Immunocompromised patients Threshold for investigation, escalation and specialist care is lower. Severe infection/sepsis Urgent assessment, cultures when appropriate, prompt effective therapy and source control are priorities. IMPORTANT: For a real patient, 'adult dose' is not enough information. Weight, renal function, indication and severity can materially change the regimen.   15. Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) AMR occurs when microorganisms develop or acquire mechanisms that reduce susceptibility to medicines. Antibiotic exposure creates selection pressure: susceptible bacteria are inhibited while resistant organisms can survive and multiply. Drivers of resistance • Unnecessary antibiotic use. • Incorrect antibiotic choice. • Incorrect dose or interval. • Poor-quality/substandard medicines. • Poor adherence or inappropriate discontinuation. • Routine use of broad-spectrum or Reserve agents when narrower options are effective. • Transmission of resistant organisms in healthcare and communities. • Poor infection prevention, sanitation and hygiene. Stewardship actions • Confirm that an antibiotic is indicated. • Obtain cultures before treatment when clinically appropriate and without unsafe delay. • Choose the narrowest effective agent when evidence allows. • Use the correct dose, route and duration. • Review therapy when microbiology results become available. • De-escalate or stop therapy when appropriate. • Document indication and duration. • Educate patients not to self-medicate or share antibiotics. WHO and Uganda Ministry of Health both emphasize antimicrobial stewardship and appropriate antibiotic use as strategies to reduce resistance. citeturn0search11turn2search1   16. Antibiotic Stewardship in Pharmacy Practice Pharmacist/dispensing professional checklist • Confirm prescription completeness and legality under local policy. • Verify patient identity and age/weight where relevant. • Ask about allergies and previous serious reactions. • Check indication and whether antibiotic therapy is clinically plausible. • Check dose, frequency, route, duration and formulation. • Check renal/hepatic function where relevant. • Screen for interactions and contraindications. • Check expiry date, batch number, packaging integrity and storage. • Give clear instructions using simple language. • Use teach-back: ask the patient to explain how they will take the medicine. • Explain common adverse effects and emergency warning signs. • Explain storage and what to do with leftover/expired medicine. • Document interventions and communicate with the prescriber when clarification is required. Teach-back example Instead of asking, 'Do you understand?', ask: 'Just so I know I explained it clearly, can you tell me how you will take these capsules and what you will do if you miss a dose?'   17. Food/water Follow the label instructions about food and take tablets/capsules with water unless told otherwise. Missed dose Do not automatically double the next dose. IMPORTANT: Educational only - not a substitute for prescription, label, guideline, or pharmacist/doctor assessment.

1. Executive overview

Analgesics are medicines used to relieve pain. They include non-opioid analgesics such as paracetamol, NSAIDs, and opioid analgesics. Paracetamol mainly analgesic and antipyretic; little anti-inflammatory. NSAIDs provide analgesic, antipyretic and anti-inflammatory by inhibiting COX. Opioids act at opioid receptors reserved for moderate-to-severe pain because of sedation, respiratory depression, dependence.

4. Paracetamol details

Adult 500mg: 1-2 tablets per dose at least 4h apart; max 8x500mg/24h. 1g: one tablet per dose, max four 1g tablets/24h. Do not combine multiple paracetamol-containing products. Liver disease, low body weight, heavy alcohol, malnutrition require lower dose. Overdose can cause severe liver injury fatal. FDA: max 4000mg/day adults 12+.

5. NSAIDs

Use lowest effective dose shortest duration. Avoid two systemic NSAIDs together. Risks: dyspepsia, gastritis, ulcer, GI bleeding, renal impairment, fluid retention, CV events. Caution elderly, kidney disease, HF, HTN, ulcer history, anticoagulant use. Interactions: anticoagulants, corticosteroids, antidepressants, antihypertensives, diuretics. Pregnancy requires review; generally avoided unless advised.

6. Aspirin

Higher doses pain relief, low doses antiplatelet. Pain: 300mg 1-3 tablets per dose at least 4h apart max 4 doses/day. Low-dose 75mg OD not stronger painkiller; antiplatelet only. Do not start/stop low-dose without advice. Do not give to children under 16 unless prescribed.

7. Opioids

Reserved for selected moderate-severe pain. Dosing individualized. IMPORTANT: Never provide generic opioid dose as universal. Requires assessment, monitoring, interaction review, secure storage.

8-11. Prescription, Expiry, Storage

Prescription must have patient, medicine, strength, form, dose, route, frequency, duration, prescriber per law. Check allergies, age, pregnancy, renal/hepatic, current meds, duplicate ingredients. Check OTC/pharmacy/Rx/controlled Uganda framework. Expiry EXP is manufacturer limit unopened. Beyond-use date after opened/reconstituted. Never use after expiry. Do not guess expiry from manufacturing date. Reconstituted liquids shorter BUD write date. Do not use if appearance deteriorated. Expiry not universal years must read package. This guide does not invent expiry dates.

12-15. Interactions, Counseling, Red flags

Paracetamol + cold/flu product → overdose. Ibuprofen + naproxen/aspirin → duplication. NSAID + anticoagulant → bleeding. NSAID + corticosteroid → GI injury. Tramadol + serotonergic antidepressants → serotonin syndrome. Tramadol/opioid + alcohol/sedatives → CNS/respiratory depression. Always check active ingredients not brand. Counseling: confirm identity, pain, severity, duration, red-flags, allergies, meds, supplements, herbal. Explain dose, interval, route, max daily, food, duplicate warning, adverse effects, urgent help, storage away children, teach-back. Red flags: severe/sudden pain, chest pain, severe abdominal/headache, trauma/fracture, persistent fever, worsening pain, vomiting blood, black stools GI bleeding, breathing difficulty, facial swelling, rash anaphylaxis, drowsiness slow breathing inability wake after opioid, overdose paracetamol/opioids, new neuro symptoms, weakness, confusion.

16-19. Safety rules

Never exceed labeled dose. Never assume two brands different actives. Do not combine systemic NSAIDs unless instructed. Keep opioids secure never share. Check expiry/storage. Use exact measuring device liquids. Ask pharmacist/doctor when pregnancy, chronic disease, multiple medicines, severe pain or overdose. Dispose expired via pharmacy/waste pathway.
